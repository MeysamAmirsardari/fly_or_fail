       robust, distributed architecture for the suppression

                                       of feeding during escape

1                                                             Abstract

2               Survival demands instant prioritization of escape over maintenance. To decode the dy-
3           namic logic embedded in the static Drosophila connectome (FlyWire v783), we simulated
4           the conflict between predator-evasion, feeding, and grooming to uncover the logic of this
5           switch. We show that escape is a holistic state defined by distributed robustness: it is
6           reliably triggered by only a fraction of visual inputs (LC4/LPLC2) and induces a system-
7           wide pause on behaviors like feeding and grooming. Within this global suppression, we
8           identify a specialized, fail-safe architecture for arresting feeding. A redundant ensemble of
9           neurons (DNge031/CB0565) targets the premotor center Roundup, effectively disfacilitating
10          motor output. Crucially, this inhibition is modular, anatomically distinct from grooming
11          suppression, and relies on additive logic to ensure suppression even if individual components
12          fail. Our results demonstrate how connectome topology implements robust, survival-critical
13          control through distributed neural architecture.

14   Introduction

15   To survive in a complex environment, animals must continuously prioritize mutually exclusive be-

16   haviors, executing high-priority actions such as predator evasion while suppressing maintenance

17   drives such as feeding [1, 2]. A central tenet of neuroethology attributes this rapid switching to

18   ”command neurons”—single critical nodes whose activation is both necessary and sufficient to

19   trigger a specific behavioral program [3–5]. This reductionist paradigm spans diverse species.

20   For example, the Giant Fiber (GF) system in Drosophila [6, 7], the Mauthner cells in fish [4],

21   and lateral giant neurons in crayfish [8] are all classically defined as dedicated decision nodes

22   for escape. Similarly, in mammals, specific hypothalamic populations such as AgRP neurons

23   are often modeled as centralized switches that drive feeding [9]. However, this focus on single-

24   node control presents an engineering paradox. Biological systems display remarkable resilience

25   to noise and damage [10]. Such holistic stability is incompatible with architectures reliant on

26   a single critical node [11, 12]. Theoretical work on biological complexity suggests that robust-

27   ness arises not from singular controls but from degeneracy—the ability of structurally distinct

28   elements to perform the same function [13]—and distributed population codes [14]. Despite

29   these theoretical insights, the specific synaptic architectures that implement such distributed

30   robustness within the brain remain largely unmapped.

31       The recent completion of the adult Drosophila whole-brain connectome (FlyWire) [15, 16]

32   provides an unprecedented opportunity to resolve this paradox. By mapping every synaptic

33   connection, we can now identify distributed networks that were previously invisible to sparse

34   sampling methods. Furthermore, a static wiring diagram alone cannot predict which pathways

35   are functionally recruited during specific behaviors. This data set allows us to investigate the

36   holistic topology of neural states. Crucially, this perspective reveals that a behavior is rarely

37   executed by a solitary command. For instance, a successful escape requires not just the trigger

38   (e.g., DNp01 [6, 17]), but the co-activation of postural control (e.g., DNp04 [7]) and directional

39   steering (e.g., DNa02 [18, 19]) to form a complete motor synergy. To decode the dynamic logic

40   embedded in this static map, we apply a connectome-constrained spiking neural network (SNN)

41   model of the entire brain [20]. This full-brain simulation approach enables us to perform ”virtual

42   stress tests” on the network—systematically perturbing inputs and performing combinatorial

43   silencing of hidden layers to reveal the redundancy and stability of behavioral states. This allows

44   us to dissect fault-tolerant architectures that are difficult to probe via single-gene manipulations

45   in vivo. This framework allows us to ask not just how a behavior is triggered, but how the

46   system ensures its execution is robust against internal variability and external perturbations.

47       In this paper, we simulate the competition between predator evasion and maintenance circuits

48   including feeding and grooming to study the circuit logic of high-priority behavioral switching.

49   We reveal that the escape response is not a rigid reflex driven by isolated lines, but a holistic

50   neural state protected by distributed stability at both input and output levels. First, we show

51   that escape is reliably initiated by activating only a fraction of the looming-sensitive population

52   (LC4/LPLC2) [7,21,22], demonstrating that danger detection relies on robust population coding

53   rather than critical individual inputs. Second, we identify a specialized, fail-safe architecture that

54   enforces behavioral exclusivity. We find that the escape circuit suppresses feeding not through

55   lateral inhibition at the motor output (MN9), but via upstream disfacilitation. A redundant

56   ensemble of descending (DNge031) and central (CB0565) neurons collectively inhibits Roundup,

57   the primary upstream of NM9, effectively cutting off the feeding drive at its source. This

58   architecture offers a distinct control advantage: by suppressing the premotor drive rather than

59   competing at the muscle level, the system avoids metabolic waste and prevents ”jittery” motor

60   conflicts, ensuring a clean state transition. By removing the excitatory input from Roundup

61   rather than inhibiting the motor neuron MN9 directly, this mechanism prevents conflicting

62   signals from competing at the output stage, ensuring a efficient behavioral switch. Perturbation

63   analysis reveals that this inhibition operates on an additive logic: the failure of any single

64   inhibitor is insufficient to release the feeding drive, establishing a redundant safety margin

65   that guarantees the absolute priority of survival. Finally, we demonstrate that this inhibitory

66   architecture is highly modular. While escape induces a system-wide pause, it utilizes distinct

67   mechanisms for different behaviors: the disfacilitation of Roundup is specific to feeding, whereas

68   grooming is suppressed via separate, dedicated gating pathways. Our findings demonstrate that

69   the connectome is engineered for systemic robustness, where stable behavioral switching emerges

70   from the synergistic action of distributed neural assemblies.

83    Holistic Activation of Escape

84    we simulated a ”looming predator” by activating the visual projection neurons LC4 and LPLC2.

85    Biological studies have established that these neurons act as specific feature detectors, summing

86    their inputs to drive the GF membrane potential [7]. Accordingly, we drove these inputs at

87    high frequencies (150 Hz) to mimic the peak synaptic drive generated during the terminal

88    phase of a rapid loom. Biologically, escape is not a singular muscle twitch but a coordinated

89    motor synergy involving takeoff triggering, postural adjustment, and directional steering. Our

90    simulation accurately captured this holistic activation pattern across varying input frequencies

91    (Fig1a).

92        In response, the model GF (DNp01) reliably entered a high-frequency firing state (> 150Hz).

93    While the biological GF typically triggers escape via a single action potential [7,17], its synapses

94    are known to possess a high safety factor capable of following high-frequency stimulation [6].

95    Within our LIF framework, this sustained output represents a robust suprathreshold state,

96    signifying a maximal excitatory drive that guarantees the ”one-for-one” transmission required

 97   for instantaneous takeoff.

98        Crucially, we observed that the downstream activity was not confined to this primary

99    line. The model spontaneously elicited a parallel ensemble of descending neurons, including

100   DNp04(> 100 Hz) and DNp02(> 100 Hz), which are essential for postural stabilization and flight

101   initiation [7]. High-resolution analysis reveals a precise temporal separation within this synergy

102   (Fig1b). The trigger (DNp01) and postural (DNp04) modules fire in tight synchronization with

103   an ultra-short latency (∼5 ms) to initiate takeoff. In contrast, the steering module (DNa02)

104   exhibits a distinct delay, activating at∼22 ms. This temporal separation suggests that the con-

105   nectome encodes a multi-stage motor program that prioritizes immediate lift-off before engaging

114   Robust Population Coding for escape

115   To determine the minimal input requirements for reliable escape initiation, we performed sys-

116   tematic ”virtual stress tests” simulations by activating random fractions of the looming-sensitive

117   population.

118       We observed a highly non-linear response profile where activating as few as 30% of LC4

119   and LPLC2 inputs was sufficient to drive the system into a stable ”escape state” (Fig.2a). As

120   visualized in the robustness landscapes, both Trigger (DNp01) and Postural (DNp04) neurons

121   rapidly entered a broad high-activity plateau, maintaining firing rates well above the 100 Hz

122   threshold even across varying input combinations (Fig.2b, c). Similarly, the Steering module

123   (left DNa02) exhibited a robust directional bias, consistently firing above its 20 Hz threshold

124   (Fig.2d) while the ipsilateral counterpart remained silent. This broad safety margin demon-

125   strates that danger detection relies on distributed population coding: the escape response is not

126   contingent on specific individual neurons but emerges from the collective activity of the visual

127   assembly, ensuring robustness against noise and partial input loss. However, this potent and

128   easily triggered escape state poses a regulatory challenge: how does the system prevent this

129   high-gain signal from conflicting with simultaneous maintenance behaviors?

138             To find the mechanism behind this suppression, we traced the inhibitory signals of the

139   escape circuit. Anatomically, the escape circuit connects to both the motor neuron (MN9) and

140   its primary premotor driver, Roundup. However, effective suppression requires active neurons,

141   not just static connections. By plotting synaptic weight against firing rate (Fig.3b), we revealed

142   a clear functional difference. Inhibitors projecting directly to MN9 (blue crosses) were largely

143   inactive or weak though they possessed high synaptic weights. In contrast, a specific group

144   targeting Roundup, including the descending neuron DNge031 and central neuron CB0565,

145   showed both high firing rates and strong inhibitory weights (marked red circles). This active

146   inhibition silences Roundup, effectively cutting off the excitatory signals to MN9. This analysis

147   exposes a critical limitation of purely static connectomics: anatomical connectivity does not

148   equal functional inhibition. Therefore, the escape circuit enforces behavioral exclusivity via

149   upstream disfacilitation, rather than directly inhibiting the motor neuron.

150   Inhibitory architecture is highly robust and modular

151   To quantify the robustness of this suppression, we performed ”counterfactual” simulations by

152   silencing the identified inhibitors (DNge031 and CB0565) individually and in combination. We

153   found that the system follows a strict additive logic (Fig.4b). Silencing any single inhibitor

154   failed to restore feeding. Even silencing two out of three inhibitors resulted in only partial

155   recovery (∼ 40 Hz), which remained significantly below the sugar-driven baseline (∼ 86 Hz).

156   This safety margin ensures fail-safe termination of feeding, as the failure of any single component

157   is insufficient to break the blockade.

158       Finally, we asked whether this architecture represents a global ”freeze” or a targeted control

159   policy. By comparing the feeding circuit with the grooming circuit, we found distinct, modular

160   strategies (Fig.4a). While escape pauses both behaviors, it targets them at different hierarchical

161   levels. Feeding is cut off at the source via upstream disfacilitation of Roundup. In contrast,

162   the grooming premotor center (aBN1) remains active during escape, but its downstream output

163   (aDN) is blocked via downstream gating. This functional dissociation confirms that the escape

164   state acts through modular control policies, applying specific stopping mechanisms adapted to

165   different behavioral conflicts.



166   Discussion

167   From Static Maps to Dynamic Logic

168   The FlyWire connectome provides a static map of the brain’s hardware. However, understanding

169   behavioral logic requires dynamics. By applying a biophysical LIF model to this structure, we

170   successfully recapitulated emergent properties, such as the temporal separation between ballistic

171   takeoff and directional steering, which are not explicitly visible in the wiring diagram alone. This

172   validates that connectome-constrained modeling is a sufficient and powerful tool to bridge the

173   gap between structural biology and system dynamics.


174   Anatomy Does Not Equate to Function

175   A critical insight from our conflict simulation is that anatomical connectivity does not equate

176   to functional efficacy. Although the connectome contains strong inhibitory connections directly

177   to the motor neuron (MN9), our model reveals that these pathways remain functionally silent

178   during escape. Instead, the brain selectively recruits a specific, redundant ensemble to target the

179   premotor source (Roundup). This finding serves as a crucial caution against inferring function

180   solely from synaptic weights without considering dynamic state variables.


181   Computational Predictions Guide Experimental Dissection

182   Finally, this work illustrates the unique value of “virtual genetics.” In biological experiments,

183   identifying redundant mechanisms is notoriously difficult because silencing a single neuron often

184   yields no phenotype, leading to false negatives. Our computational approach enabled us to

185   perform combinatorial perturbations that are technically difficult in vivo, revealing the hidden

186   fault-tolerant architecture of the feeding suppression circuit. These predictions provide precise

187   coordinates for future research: rather than searching for a single ”command neuron” that

188   stops feeding, experimentalists should investigate distributed ensembles. By illuminating these

189   invisible architectural principles, computational connectomics provides the necessary blueprint

190   to guide the dissection of complex biological systems.



191   Methods

192   Connectome Data and Neuron Selection

193   All neuronal reconstruction and synaptic connectivity data were obtained from the FlyWire full-

194   brain connectome (Codex version 783). Neurons of interest (e.g., LC4, LPLC2, DNp01, MN9)

195   were identified using cell-type annotations in the FlyWire Codex.


196   Computational Model

197   We implemented a Leaky Integrate-and-Fire (LIF) [20, 26] spiking neural network model of the

198   entire central brain, following the framework established by Shiu et al. [20]. The dynamics of

199   the membrane potential Vi for each neuron i are governed by the following differential equations:

                                                     dVi   gi − (Vi − Vresting )
                                                         =                                                                        (1)
                                                      dt          Tmbr



                                                               dgi    gi
                                                                   =−                                                             (2)
                                                               dt     τ

200   where gi represents the synaptic conductance resulting from the aggregate firing of presynaptic

201   neurons. Upon the arrival of a spike from presynaptic neuron j, the conductance is updated


                                                                    11
  bioRxiv preprint doi: https://doi.org/10.64898/2025.12.14.694122; this version posted December 16, 2025. The copyright holder for this
     preprint (which was not certified by peer review) is the author/funder, who has granted bioRxiv a license to display the preprint in
                                    perpetuity. It is made available under a CC-BY 4.0 International license.




202   according to the rule: gi ← gi + wj,i . When Vi reaches the firing threshold Vthreshold , the neuron

203   spikes, and Vi is reset to Vreset for a refractory period Trefractory .

204       Biophysical parameters were fixed to values established in previous Drosophila modeling

205   studies [20, 27]:

206       • Resting potential: Vresting = −52 mV

207       • Reset potential: Vreset = −52 mV

208       • Firing threshold: Vthreshold = −45 mV

209       • Membrane time constant: Tmbr = 20 ms (derived from membrane resistance Rmbr =

210          10 kΩ · cm2 and capacitance Cmbr = 2µF · cm−2 )

211       • Synapse decay time constant: τ = 5 ms

212       • Refractory period: Trefractory = 2.2 ms

213       • Synaptic delay: Tdly = 1.8 ms

214       The synaptic weight wj,i connecting neuron j to neuron i is defined as:



                                                       wj,i = Nsyn · S · Wsyn                                                     (3)

215   where Nsyn is the number of ultrastructural synapses from j to i derived from the FlyWire

216   connectome, S is the neurotransmitter sign (+1 for excitatory, −1 for inhibitory) predicted

217   by the connectome-wide classifier [23], and Wsyn = 0.275 mV is the global scaling parameter

218   representing the postscynaptic potential change per synapse.


219   Simulation strategy

220   Sensory inputs (Visual looming, Sugar) were modeled as Poisson spike trains delivered to specific

221   receptor neurons (LC4/LPLC2, Sugar GRNs). ”Counterfactual” silencing was performed via

222   ”virtual axotomy”: the outgoing synaptic weights of specific target neurons (e.g., DNge031) were

223   set to zero, effectively removing their influence on the network while preserving their internal

224   dynamics. All simulations were performed using the Brian2 simulator.
