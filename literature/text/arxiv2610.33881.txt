                                            Abstract
                                            Connectomes now map the neurons and synapses of an adult fly’s brain and nerve cord, but how
                                            much of behaviour follows from the wiring is unknown. Here we turn one such connectome into
                                            a spiking network of 172,433 neurons with a published brain model’s untuned parameters, and
                                            run it alone and inside a simulated body. Two fixed rules join them: odours and looming objects
                                            drive sensory neurons, and motor neurons move the legs. Alone, the network passes a signal to
                                            the motor neurons it is known to drive when the pathway supplies about 2% or more of their
                                            input. In the body, the fly navigates around obstacles to 95% of the odour sources. A rewiring
                                            that keeps each neuron’s connection counts and signs reaches 18%, and the fly’s leg commands
                                            replayed with the senses off reach almost none, so navigation needs the measured wiring and
                                            sensory feedback. Dopamine-gated weakening of the mushroom body’s output synapses turns
                                            approach to an odour into avoidance, which requires the pairing and is odour-specific once one
                                            olfactory synapse class is scaled. An untrained connectome thus supports a behaviour and its
                                            learned reversal, and the pathways it cannot drive mark what the diagram leaves out.


                                            Introduction
                                            Complete wiring diagrams exist for the nematode[1, 2] and for the brain of the fly larva[3].
                                            Electron-microscopy volumes of the adult fly, and the reconstructions made from them, cover a
                                            large part of the central brain[4], the whole brain[5, 6, 7] and the ventral nerve cord[8, 9, 10].
                                            The descending and ascending neurons that join brain and nerve cord have been matched across
                                            those datasets[11]. The wiring of the adult’s nervous system is now known at the resolution of
                                            single synapses. Two connectomes now reconstruct the brain and the nerve cord of one animal
                                            together: the Brain And Nerve Cord connectome (BANC) of a female[12] and a male’s[13]. In
                                            them a path can be followed from a sensory neuron to a motor neuron within one dataset. A
                                            connectome records which neurons are connected and by how many synapses, and each neuron’s
                                            transmitter can be predicted from the images[14]. It does not record the dynamics of the neurons
                                            or their modulation[15, 16].
                                            How much of a nervous system’s function follows from its wiring is an open question[15, 17, 18, 19,
                                            20]. A spiking model built from the catalogued cell types of the protocerebral bridge reproduced
                                            the ring-attractor dynamics of the fly’s heading signal over a wide range of parameters[21]. A
                                            leaky integrate-and-fire model of the fly brain, with its parameters fixed in advance and its
                                            weights taken from the connectome, predicted which neurons drive feeding and grooming, and
                                            experiments confirmed the predictions[22]. Simulations of the nerve cord’s connectome identified
                                            a small circuit that generates the walking rhythm[23]. Networks that take their connectivity
                                            from the optic-lobe connectome, with the remaining parameters optimised for a task, predict the


                                                                                               1
responses of identified visual neurons[19]. Other results set limits. A connectome often leaves
the dynamics of a recurrent network under-determined when the properties of the neurons are
unknown[24, 20], and in the nematode the measured propagation of signals departs from what
the anatomy predicts[25]. One way to measure what the wiring explains is to fit nothing: run the
connectome as it is read, and record where it reproduces known biology and where it fails. Many
such tests are possible in the fly, because the behavioural roles of single cell types are known.
Activating most descending neurons drives a stereotyped behaviour[26]. Particular descending
neurons turn the walking fly[27, 28] or make it walk backward[29], looming detectors of the
optic lobe drive the giant fibre that triggers the escape take-off[30, 31], and the mushroom body
supports olfactory learning[32, 33] through depression of its output synapses[34].
Behaviour adds a second requirement. An animal’s movements change what its senses receive, and
behaviour emerges from the interaction of the nervous system, the body and the environment[35,
36], so a nervous system can be tested as a controller only inside a body. An open project is
building an integrative simulation of the whole nematode[37]. Physics-based models of the fly’s
body exist[36, 38, 39]. Their controllers have been written by hand or trained by reinforcement
learning[38, 39], including one that takes the brain connectome as its architecture and learns to
control the body[40]. What the measured wiring of the brain and nerve cord contributes when it
is run untrained in such a body, inside a closed sensorimotor loop, is the question we address.
Here we build a spiking network of 172,433 neurons from the BANC connectome, with every
parameter of the published brain model[22] kept at its published value, and test it in three settings.
Outside the body, we drive 15 pathways whose behavioural role is known from experiments,
under criteria written down before each test. A pathway conducts when it supplies about 2% or
more of its target’s input, and the failures trace to features a wiring diagram lacks: an electrical
synapse, the absence of ongoing activity. In the same setting the olfactory pathway as read
gives a dense odour code, and scaling the output of one class of antennal-lobe neuron brings it
into the range measured in the fly. In the NeuroMechFly body[38], coupled through two fixed
rules, the network reaches 95% of 32 odour sources across nine arenas, where a network rewired
to keep every neuron’s degrees and signs reaches 18%. Lesion and rescue experiments place
the behavioural deficit at the output of the mushroom body, and replaying the same motor
commands without sensory feedback reaches 2% of the sources, so the closed loop is required.
Dopamine-gated depression of the mushroom-body output synapses while the fly forages shifts
the balance of that output from approach to avoidance, and the trained fly turns away from the
odour. The depression is associative when Kenyon-cell activity depends on the odour.


Results

The connectome as a spiking network inside a body

We built a spiking model of the BANC connectome, the electron-microscopy reconstruction of an
adult female’s brain and ventral nerve cord[12]: 172,433 neurons, 11.75 million connections and
35.7 million synapses. Each neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire unit with the parameters of Shiu
et al.[22], and each connection is signed by its presynaptic neuron’s predicted transmitter[14]
(Methods). Every parameter keeps its published value; none was fitted to our tests. The network
runs inside the NeuroMechFly body[36, 38] under a fixed interface of two rules (Fig. 1a; Methods).
The arena’s odour and looming set the rates of the olfactory receptor neurons and of the LC
and LPLC visual projection neurons. The six leg motor-neuron pools set the gait, with the turn
scaled by the output of the mushroom-body output neurons (MBONs) and by the DNa02 pair.
We call the balance between the MBONs that drive approach and those that drive avoidance the
valence, because in the fly the MBONs encode the valence of an odour[41, 42, 43]. The interface
and its constants are the same in every experiment.

The 24 regions we read from follow the connectome’s labels (Fig. 1b, d; Methods). A 60 ms pulse
of odour at the receptor neurons reaches the lateral horn and the descending neurons within
12 ms of its onset, the mushroom body within 18 ms and the leg motor pools within 24 ms
(Supplementary Fig. 1). A looming pulse at the visual projection neurons reaches the descending
neurons within 6 ms and the leg pools within 18 ms. The order in which neurons first spike
follows the order in which a traversal of the wiring from the stimulated neurons reaches them
(Spearman ρ = 0.59 over the 6,775 neurons that spike after the odour pulse and 0.58 over the
3,884 after the looming pulse; Supplementary Fig. 2), and those neurons are 4% and 2% of the
ones the traversal reaches.
Structure and function connect the regions with different densities. Of the 576 ordered pairs
of the 24 regions (each region onto every region, itself included), 358 are joined by at least one
synapse and 96 by more than 10,000 (Fig. 1c, e). The largest blocks lie within a region. The
descending neurons make 1.53 million synapses, 0.65 million of them onto nerve-cord intrinsic
neurons. Driving each region alone at 40 Hz and reading the spiking of every other region gives
a sparser map: 139 of the 552 region pairs respond at 0.1 Hz per neuron or more (Fig. 1f). The
strongest responses are olfactory input onto the mushroom body (33 Hz per neuron) and the
lateral horn (22 Hz), other central-brain input onto the mushroom body (29 Hz) and fan-shaped
body input onto the noduli (30 Hz). Seven regions evoke no response elsewhere. Three are the
noduli, the protocerebral bridge and the rest of the central complex: 71 to 100% of their synapses
onto other regions come from cells signed inhibitory, which lower nothing in a silent network. The
other four are the motor groups, which supply no region with as much as 0.1% of its input. The
map follows the excitatory-signed synapse counts between the regions at ρ = 0.65 over the 334
region pairs with any synapse, and the counts regardless of sign at 0.56 (Supplementary Fig. 3);
the strongest responses with no direct synapse, the receptors’ onto the mushroom body and the
lateral horn, are relayed through the antennal lobe. Six regions are never reached (the optic lobe
and five sensory groups, which receive almost no central input). The map is near-deterministic:
the seed-to-seed coefficient of variation is 0 at the median and above 0.01 in 83 of the 576 cells.


Pathways of known behavioural role conduct in the untrained network when
they carry enough of their target’s input

Before putting the network in a body we asked whether it reproduces pathways whose behavioural
role is known from experiments (Fig. 2a, b; Methods). We drove each population outside the
body and read an identified target, with the metric and threshold of every test written down
before it ran. Every test has an undriven control and a twin on a rewired network that keeps
each neuron’s degrees and signs and shuffles its partners, so a result counts only if it conducts
and dies under the shuffle.
Taste conducts and is suppressed[22]. MN9 is the motor neuron of the muscle that protracts the
rostrum of the proboscis[44], and in the animal it is excited by sugar and inhibited by bitter
compounds[45]. The labellar sugar receptor neurons supply 7.6% of MN9’s 4,608 input synapses
through 38 relay neurons. Driving them fires MN9 at 1.9 Hz at 200 Hz drive and not at 100 Hz
or below. The rewired twin gives 0 Hz at every rate (Fig. 2c). Adding bitter receptor drive at 100
and 200 Hz lowers the response to 0.76 and 0.02 Hz, and Ir94e drive lowers it to 0 Hz at either
rate. Water, the control, does not lower it (2.6 and 2.4 Hz). No tastant drives MN9 on its own,
as in the animal[22]. Shuffling only the tastant’s outgoing connections lifts the suppression (1.93
and 1.41 Hz at 100 Hz bitter and Ir94e; P = 0.0008 and 0.0002 over nine seeds) (Fig. 2g). We
had registered the suppression as expected to fail, because the bitter path onto MN9 is mostly
excitatory at one hop; it held.
Escape follows the synapse counts onto the giant fibre, the descending neuron whose spike
triggers the fast take-off[46]. LPLC2, which supplies 6.5% of the giant fibre’s input, drives it

at 44.7 Hz, and LC4 with LPLC2 (8.7%) at 47.7 Hz, with the rewired twin at 0 Hz in both
cases[30, 31, 47]. LC4 alone (2.2%) is silent at 40 Hz drive and conducts from 80 Hz (0.5, 9.6
and 19.6 Hz above control at 80, 150 and 250 Hz; twin 0 Hz). LC6, LC15 and LPLC1 drive
jumping in the animal[48] but make 0 to 2 synapses onto the giant fibre and do not drive it, nor
does LC11, the control (Fig. 2d). The giant fibre’s own output onto the tergotrochanteral motor
neuron is 13 chemical synapses, 0.5% of that neuron’s input, and it does not conduct. In the
animal the motor neuron is coupled to the giant fibre by gap junctions[49] that accompany the
chemical synapses[50]. The motor neuron follows the giant fibre once one connection is added by
hand to stand for the electrical part: from 104 synapse equivalents (7.9% of its input, 19 Hz),
and one for one from 208 (Fig. 2f). Every pathway tested onto an identified neuron conducts at
some drive if it supplies at least 2% of that neuron’s input, and the seven supplying 0.03% or
less do not (Fig. 2f).
Descending control lateralises in the wiring alone. DNa02 turns the walking fly toward the side
on which it is activated[27, 28]. Driving one DNa02 at 600 Hz, one cell per side, lateralises the
leg-motor drive to the driven side (asymmetry +0.71 on the left, −1.00 on the right), and the
rewired twin does not (Fig. 2e). PFL3, the central-complex steering output (8 and 17 cells per
side)[51], lateralises the same way (+0.61, −0.39), and PFL1, the control, never reaches the
cord. DNg12 (45 cells), the grooming command[52], recruits the front-leg pools (24.6 Hz against
0.008 and 0.029 Hz in the middle and hind pools; twin 0.13 Hz). DNa01[49, 28] lateralises only
from the left in the silent network (asymmetry +0.49; the right cell drives no leg motor neuron).
MDN, the backward-walking command[29], drives about eight leg motor neurons, in the front
pools and the right hind pool (330 spikes in 3 s; the twin 92 spikes in one neuron). The leg
pools carry no direction, and the gait rule turns their rates into forward stepping, so whether the
walking would be backward cannot be read in either setting. DNp09, which initiates forward
walking[53, 54], gives two spikes of leg drive. LC16, whose retreat runs through MDN[55], gives
no leg drive at 40, 600 or 1,500 Hz, and LC10a[56] conducts but survives the shuffle.
A background shows what the silent network cannot. LPLC2 still drives the giant fibre with
independent 2 to 10 Hz input to every neuron (24.5, 14.2 and 5.2 Hz above control at 2, 5 and
10 Hz), and DNg12 still drives the front legs (9.8, 8.5 and 6.1 Hz, 94 to 100% of it in the front
pools). The sugar response falls to 0.35 Hz at 2 Hz and no longer clears its guard. At 20 Hz only
DNg12 still does (Fig. 2i). On that background the steering neurons act by suppression. At 2 Hz,
DNa01 on the left lowers the drive to its own side by 1.91 Hz and raises the other side by 0.82
Hz (twin 0.06 Hz). DNa02 on the left lowers its own side by 1.35 Hz and on the right by 1.30 Hz
(twins 0.02 and 0.15 Hz); every seed falls below its control, but each misses the registered guard
of three control standard deviations, 1.35 Hz, by 0.05 Hz or less. Bilateral DNa02 lowers the
total by 2.27 Hz (twin +0.54). The animal shows the same sign: DNa02 shortens the strides of
the legs on its own side, and its bilateral activation slows walking[27]. DNp09 clears its guard at
2 Hz with suppression (2.21 Hz below control; twin −0.20), the opposite of the sign measured in
the animal, and so does MDN (1.70 Hz below; twin +0.69). Of the 15 pathways, seven conduct,
die under the shuffle and carry the sign measured in the animal: sugar, bitter, LPLC2, LC4 with
LPLC2, DNa02, PFL3 and DNg12 (Fig. 2h). Three do so conditionally: the giant fibre’s output
with the gap junction; DNa01 on a background, from the left; DNp09 on a background, with the
wrong sign. One, MDN, conducts and dies under the shuffle but has no direction the model can
read. Four fail the test: leg sugar, which supplies 0.02% of MN9’s input; LC4 alone at 40 Hz;
LC16; LC10a.

The antennal lobe as read is bistable, and one synapse class sets the Kenyon-cell
code

The 2,978 receptor neurons reach 616 of the 699 projection neurons through 203,937 synapses
and the antennal lobe’s 429 local neurons through 172,141 (Fig. 3a). The projection neurons
return 217,657 synapses onto the local neurons and send 168,315 onto the 4,504 Kenyon cells.
The Kenyon cells’ 28,415 synapses onto the 104 MBONs are the ones the learning of Fig. 6
changes. The local neurons are, by predicted transmitter, 161 glutamatergic, 146 GABAergic, 105
cholinergic, 14 serotonergic and 3 octopaminergic (Fig. 3b). The cholinergic class makes the most
synapses: a median of 3,029 output synapses per cell, against 456 for the GABAergic and 304
for the glutamatergic cells. In the fly the local neurons are diverse in transmitter[57]: many are
GABAergic[58], a third are glutamatergic and inhibit[59], and excitatory ones exist[60, 61]. The
model has the APL neuron, the feedback that sparsens the Kenyon-cell code in the animal[62]:
two cells, one of them without a label in the neuron table, with 22,926 synapses onto the Kenyon
cells. Both are predicted cholinergic, so in the model they excite the Kenyon cells, although the
APL is GABAergic in the fly[63] and GABA is the transmitter the connectome’s own annotation
lists as verified for it (Methods). Silencing the labelled APL lowers the share of Kenyon cells
active, as read, from 68% to 61%.
The network as read gives a dense code. We presented two odours of five disjoint glomeruli each
(Fig. 3c). Either odour fires 68% of the Kenyon cells, and the two codes’ 5% most active cells
overlap by 0.90. The projection-neuron layer is already 74% active, with an overlap of 0.86,
and the MBONs fire about 2,400 spikes per presentation whatever the odour. In the fly, 5 to
10% of Kenyon cells respond to an odour[64, 65, 62]. The cholinergic local neurons’ output onto
the projection neurons sets the density. Scaling it by a gain g from 1 to 0 lowers the share of
Kenyon cells active through 43%, 15%, 6.1%, 3.4%, 2.7% and 2.3% to 1.8%, and the share of
projection neurons active from 74% to 10% (Fig. 3d). The overlap falls from 0.90 to 0.24 at
g = 0.1 before rising again in the near-silent code. At g = 0.15 and 0.1 the code lies in the
fly’s window with an overlap under 0.5, and g = 0.15 is the matched variant used from here on.
Signing every local neuron inhibitory instead gives 1.4% of the Kenyon cells active, below the
window (Supplementary Table 4). Over eight odours the share of Kenyon cells active is 68 to
70% as read and 4.9 to 17.2% in the matched variant, median 6.8% (Fig. 3e). The matched
MBON output is 52 to 287 spikes per presentation, against 2,352 to 2,483 as read (Fig. 3f). Part
of the matched output does not pass through the Kenyon cells. For the first odour pair, the 52
spikes to odour A (the median over six seeds) all come from one MBON05 cell. The unlabelled
APL cell drives that cell through 62 synapses, while the Kenyon cells supply 154 of its 468 input
synapses, so depression of the Kenyon-cell synapses cannot remove that output.
The dense code comes from the loop between the projection and local neurons. The as-read
network fires 67% of its Kenyon cells after the warm-up with no receptor input at all, 68% at 5
Hz and 77% at 120 Hz. It fires none when started from silence. Receptor input at 5 Hz switches
it on, and it then stays on (67% once the input is removed). The matched variant follows its
input: 4.2%, 8.3%, 43% and 68% at 0, 5, 40 and 120 Hz, and 4.2% again after (Fig. 3g). Its
code stays sparse for odours of up to ten glomeruli (6.0%, 16% and 35% at 5, 10 and 20; as
read 68%, 70% and 72%). Halving or quartering the odour drive leaves the density unchanged
(matched 5.3% and 5.1%) and raises the matched overlap (0.55, 0.73) while lowering its output
(Fig. 3k). We silenced the cholinergic types one at a time (Fig. 3i, j). Silencing lLN1_bc (34
cells, 105,956 output synapses) lowers the share of Kenyon cells active from 68% to 50%, and
the other ten types leave 66 to 68% active. The fall scales with the type’s output (Spearman
ρ = 0.78, P = 0.0045, 11 types). Types silenced together do more than the sum of their single
falls. The three largest together leave 25% of the Kenyon cells active where the sum of their
single falls predicts 46%. All eleven leave 3% against a predicted 40%, and all 105 cells 2%.
Training on odour A in the matched variant depresses A’s MBON output and, in part, the

The connectome in the body navigates around obstacles to odour sources, and
the navigation needs the connectome’s measured wiring

Twenty flies, each a seed with its own input and initial conditions, walked nine arenas holding 32
odour sources behind walls of increasing clutter, under three wirings: intact, rewired, and with
the mushroom body lesioned (Fig. 4a to d). The intact fly reached 30.25 of the 32 sources per
fly (95%). It reached every source of its arena in 161 of 180 runs and at least one in every run
(Fig. 4f, g). The rewired fly reached 5.80 (18%; no source in 92 of its 180 runs, every source in 2)
and the lesioned fly 3.45 (no source in 117 runs, every source in none). A correlated random
walk at the fly’s own speed reached 2.20. No rewired fly reached as many sources as any intact
fly (at most 11 against at least 27 of 32). The rewired fly reaches more than the lesioned one (1
to 11 against 1 to 6 per fly; Mann–Whitney P = 0.0099, Cliff’s δ = +0.48, 95% interval +0.14
to +0.76). Its MBONs still fire, 7.5 spikes per step against the intact fly’s 154, and 75 of the
104 MBONs drive approach, so the balance reads a weak positive valence (0.11 against 1.41).
For scale, a reference controller that is given the position of every source and walks straight to
the nearest at the same speed reaches 29.00 if walls stop it and 32.00 if it slides along them
(Supplementary Table 1). The intact fly’s misses are mostly in the two most cluttered arenas
(17 of the 19 runs that miss a source). In the four-wall slalom 9 of 20 runs reach all five sources
and 5 stop at the second; in the dense slalom 14 of 20 reach all four. Along a corridor the intact
fly reached the first source in every run and, having reached one, the next 99.4% of the time
(Fig. 4h). The rewired and lesioned flies reached the first in 35% and 28% of runs and went on
to the next 28.4% and 9.5% of the time. Both manipulated flies keep walking (Fig. 4i to k). The
median speed is 8.9, 12.8 and 11.8 mm/s for the intact, rewired and lesioned fly, with 97.2%,
99.6% and 99.2% of time moving, and the straightness 0.61, 0.31 and 0.61. The rewired and
lesioned flies leave the arena frame in 64% and 65% of samples. The olfactory input is the same
in all three (5.92 spikes per receptor neuron per step, 99.7% of them active).
The firing rates are the network’s own, measured during the runs (Fig. 4e, l, m). Every neuron’s
spikes were recorded in one run per condition (arena i, seed 0). In that run the intact mushroom
body fires 1.93 spikes per neuron per 50 ms step with 77% of its neurons active, and the lateral
horn 1.30. The lesion removes the mushroom body and leaves the lateral horn at 1.30. The
rewiring leaves the mushroom body silent (0.006 spikes per neuron, 2% active) and the lateral
horn at 0.018 under the same receptor drive. The descending and leg-motor rates stay near
intact in the lesioned fly and fall in the rewired one (front-leg motor 0.13, 0.03 and 0.13 spikes
per neuron per step). The MBON output, logged in every run, shows the same across all 180
runs per condition: 154 spikes per step in every intact run (153.7 to 154.9), 7.5 in the rewired fly
(3.0 to 18.0) and none in the lesioned.


The labels are causal, the deficit localises, and the loop is required

In a forage arena with three sources behind a wall (Fig. 5a to d), the intact fly reaches all three
in all 20 seeds (20 flies with their own input and initial conditions, as in the battery). With the
mushroom body lesioned it reaches none in 20 of 20. Its leg motor-neuron rate (152 against 154
Hz), wall avoidance (1.9 wall-contact steps) and path length (220 against 193 mm) are kept,

                                                 6
and its looming-driven DNa02 rate falls (0.81 against 1.99 spikes per step): a loss of approach
with walking spared. The rewired fly reaches 0.10 of 3 (18 of 20 none) and loses more: DNa02
drive 0.54 and leg motor rate 98 Hz (64% of intact). Pinning the valence scalar at 1.44, close to
the intact fly’s 1.41, restores approach in both, to 3.00 and 2.55 of 3 (20 and 17 of 20 all three).
It does not restore the rewired fly’s walking (103 Hz) or the lesioned fly’s DNa02 rate (0.77).
The behavioural deficit of both manipulations therefore lies at the mushroom-body output. The
rescue has a threshold below 0.7: pinning at 0.7 rescues the lesioned fly as fully as 1.44 does
(3.00 of 3), and the intact fly pinned at 0 reaches nothing while walking normally (leg motor rate
99% of intact). A random lesion of 4,608 neurons, the size of the mushroom-body set, leaves
approach mostly intact: 2.55 of 3, with 3 of 20 seeds reaching nothing against the lesion’s 20 of
20 (Fisher’s exact P = 2.6 × 10−8 ). It costs walking (leg motor rate 85% of intact). Cutting the
visual loop, with the looming signal set to zero, abolishes wall avoidance (233 wall-contact steps,
a nearest approach of 0.17 mm) and with it, in this walled arena, approach (0.30 of 3), while
walking is kept (99%). Supplementary Table 2 gives every measure for every condition.
Cutting the whole sensory loop abolishes navigation (Fig. 5e, g). Each closed-loop run’s own
motor commands were replayed to the body from a start rotated by 30◦ , with the network’s
output ignored. The replays reach 0.07 sources per run against 3.29 for the closed loop from the
same rotated start (2.0% against 94.0% of the sources, per fly). Of the 180 replays, 169 reach
nothing and none reaches every source, while 154 of 180 closed-loop runs reach every source. All
20 flies, all nine arenas and all 180 pairs fall the same way (W = 0, P = 1.9 × 10−6 over flies).
The rotation alone does not account for this: the rotated closed loop reaches 3.29 sources per
run against 3.36 for the unrotated record (P = 0.13).
Driving one descending neuron in the body, with no steering rule written for it, reproduces the
sign measured in the animal where the wiring did (Fig. 5f; Supplementary Table 3). DNa01 turns
the fly +116◦ from the left and −150◦ from the right against a no-drive baseline of −7◦ and +0.5
mm/s over the 2 s of drive, a mirror the rewired twins lack (+55◦ and +36◦ ). The threat-response
cluster slows it by 5.1 mm/s where, rewired, it speeds it up by 6.3. DNa02 turns the fly right
when driven bilaterally (−38◦ ; twin −7◦ ) and, within the no-drive spread, from either side alone
(−16◦ and −25◦ ). Its bilateral drive speeds the fly up (0.8 mm/s) where the animal slows[27].
DNp09 turns left (+54◦ ) and slows (2.2 mm/s) where the animal walks forward[53]. DNp01 does
nothing. DNg12 speeds the fly up by 1.8 mm/s, as does its twin (2.2), because the gait controller
reads its front-leg drive as amplitude. The sign depends on a baseline (Fig. 5j). Every drive
speeds the silent fly up with the receptor drive at 0 Hz, and the steering neurons turn it away
from the driven side (DNa01 left −219◦ , DNa02 left −269◦ and right +219◦ ), the excitation-only
regime of the silent wiring (Fig. 2). DNa01 turns the fly toward the driven side from both sides
with 40 or 120 Hz of receptor drive (+86◦ and −167◦ at 40 Hz), and DNa02 does so from the
right (−47◦ against +21◦ for no drive at 40 Hz). The threat cluster arrests the fly (5.2 and 5.1
mm/s) where at 0 Hz it accelerated it (3.1). The speed increase reported for DNp09 appears
only in the silent body (1.7 mm/s) and reverses on a baseline (−2.1 and −2.2 mm/s at 40 and
120 Hz).
Removing neurons at random degrades navigation gradually; removing them by traffic, those
with the most synapses first, degrades it abruptly (Fig. 5h, i). Random removal of 5%, 10% and
20% of the candidate neurons leaves 2.53, 2.20 and 2.50 of 3 sources, 30% leaves 1.30, 50% 0.30
and 70% 0.18, with the leg motor rate falling from 123 to 4 Hz. Removing the 1% of neurons
with the most synapses leaves no source reached, with the leg motor rate at 6 Hz, and so does
removing 2%. At those two fractions 11 and 2 of 20 runs end in a physics fault, the partly driven
body falling; none does once the legs stop (22 faults in 280 runs). Removing the top 1% within
the central brain alone (312 neurons) leaves 0.78 of 3 with the leg rate at 15% of intact, and
from 2% navigation is gone with the leg rate at zero. Ranking by traffic finds the premotor drive
before any navigation-specific core, so this test separates none from walking.

Dopamine-gated depression in the loop turns approach into avoidance, associa-
tively when the code depends on the odour

We let the Kenyon-cell-to-MBON synapses of the aversive compartments depress under a
reinforcement signal while the fly forages, with nothing else changed (Fig. 6a; Methods). The
animal learns aversive olfactory associations by the same rule[34]: dopaminergic neurons deliver
the reinforcement[67, 68], and the learned behaviour follows from a shift in the balance between
the MBONs that drive approach and those that drive avoidance[41, 43]. Six training episodes with
the odour paired with the reinforcement take the mean weight of the 8,377 plastic connections
from 0.513 to 0.138 mV. The approach-driving MBONs fall from 103 to 37 spikes per step while
the avoidance-driving MBONs stay at 50 (49 after training), so the valence goes from 1.41 to
−0.33 (Fig. 6b, c). One training episode is enough to take it there. The behaviour changes with
it. Before training every fly reaches the source. After training none of 20 does (Fig. 6d, e, g).
The trained fly turns away from the odour, and its closest approach to the source is a median
19.6 mm against 0.15 mm without plasticity (P = 6.8 × 10−8 , Cliff’s δ = +1.00 against no
plasticity, on closest approach and on devaluation). The learning rate is a dose with a threshold.
At η = 10−5 the valence after training is 0.44 and all 20 flies still reach the source; at 3 × 10−5 it
is −0.21 and none does (Fig. 6b, f). From 10−4 to 0.08 it stays between −0.31 and −0.33 while
the weights go on falling, from 0.233 to 0.134 mV. The valence levels off because the depression
is confined to its targets. In the recorded seed the 41 approach-driving MBONs of the aversive
compartments fall from 69 to 6 spikes per step. The 34 approach-driving MBONs of the other
compartments (35 to 31) and the avoidance-driving MBONs (50 to 49) keep their output.
In the network as read the depression is not associative. Reinforcement with the odour withheld
depresses the synapses almost as much (weight 0.183 mV, valence −0.29). Those flies turn away
as well: none of 20 reaches the source, and against the paired flies on closest approach P = 0.49,
δ = +0.13, 95% interval −0.25 to +0.49. The as-read Kenyon cells fire without the odour: 67%
of them at 0 Hz of receptor input, and 68% at the 5 Hz the arena supplies when the odour is
withheld (Fig. 3g). The eligibility trace is present with or without the odour, and reinforcement
alone depresses the same synapses. In the matched variant, whose Kenyon cells fire only with
the odour (4.2% at 0 Hz, 8.3% at 5 Hz, 68% at 120 Hz), the same protocol is associative at the
synapse and in the behaviour. Pairing takes the weight to 0.195 mV and the valence to −0.53,
and none of 20 trained flies reaches the source (median closest approach 21.1 mm). Reinforcement
alone leaves the weight at 0.508 mV and the valence at 1.38, and all 20 flies reach the source,
as they do without plasticity (paired against reinforcement alone, P = 6.8 × 10−8 , δ = +1.00)
(Fig. 6h, i). The registered tests, which are on the behaviour, read associative and expressed
(Methods). Both codes are dense while the odour is present (77% as read, 68% matched, every
receptor neuron driven), so the associativity of the depression follows from the code’s dependence
on the odour and does not require a sparse code.
The avoidance needs both the balance readout and the confined reinforcement; two further arms
change one of the two at a time (Supplementary Fig. 4; Supplementary Table 7). A sum over
every MBON cannot change sign. The same depression, with the valence read as the summed
spike count of every MBON and every connection plastic, lowers it from 1.44 to a floor of 0.24,
held by MBONs the Kenyon cells do not drive. Of 20 trained flies, 19 then still reach the source
(Supplementary Discussion; Supplementary Table 6 sets the two readouts side by side). With
the balance and the reinforcement in every compartment the trained fly walks past the source,
steering neither toward it nor away (valence after pairing 0.03, −0.02 in the matched variant; 2
and 1 of 20 flies reach the source). With the summed count and the confined reinforcement all
20 flies still reach it.

Discussion
We built a spiking network from a complete brain-and-nerve-cord connectome with every pa-
rameter at its published value, tested it against pathways of known behavioural role, placed
it in a body and perturbed it there. A pathway of known behavioural role conducts in the
untrained network when it supplies about 2% or more of its target’s input; those that fail supply
a hundredth of that or depend on something the wiring diagram does not record. In the body
the intact wiring reaches 30 of 32 odour sources; a rewired network with the same degrees and
signs reaches 5.8, and a random walk 2. The same motor commands replayed without sensory
feedback reach 2% of the sources. Plasticity at one class of synapse, run inside the loop, turns
the fly’s approach to an odour into avoidance, and whether the depression is associative depends
on whether Kenyon-cell activity depends on the odour.
Each outcome is a statement about the wiring, because every parameter keeps its published value.
Other connectome-based models optimise the unknown neuron and synapse parameters against
a task[69, 19], train a network against the behavioural effects of silencing identified neurons[70],
or train the connectome as a controller by reinforcement learning[40]. The body models have
been driven by controllers trained the same way[38, 39]. These approaches reach an accuracy
that an untrained model does not attempt. A trained interface can make a network drive a body
whatever its wiring: a worm connectome, read into a fly body through a decoder trained by
reinforcement learning to imitate real flies, walks like a fly[71]. Here the input and the readout
are fixed rules, and nothing between the network and the body is trained. With the same rules
the rewired network, every degree and sign kept, finds few of the sources, so the navigation comes
from the wiring. What fitting removes is the possibility of asking how much the wiring alone
explains. That question is open[15, 19]: with the neuronal and synaptic properties unknown,
a connectome often leaves the dynamics of a recurrent network under-determined[24, 20]. Our
parameters are the ones Shiu et al. used for a brain without its nerve cord[22]: values from
earlier models and recordings, with one weight set on a feeding response. Applied unchanged to
a second animal, with its nerve cord, they reproduce that model’s taste results, sugar driving the
proboscis motor neuron and bitter suppressing it, and they carry descending commands through
the neck to the leg motor neurons.
The misses have identifiable causes. In the animal the giant fibre is coupled to its motor neuron
by gap junctions[49] that accompany the chemical synapses[50]. The connectome holds only the
chemical part, and one connection added by hand restores the pathway. The steering neurons
need ongoing activity to act on. In the walking fly DNa02 shortens the strides of the legs on its
own side[27], and the steering signals of the two hemispheres change in opposite directions[28]. A
silent network cannot lower a rate that is already zero, so it shows only excitation of the driven
side, and a silent body turns away from that side. The driven side’s leg drive is suppressed in the
network with 2 Hz of background, and DNa01 turns the fly toward the driven side from either
side once the body has receptor drive. Command-like descending neurons also act in the animal
by recruiting further descending neurons[72], which may be why one cell driven alone gives part
of a behaviour. In C. elegans, measured signal propagation departs from what anatomy predicts,
in part through extrasynaptic signalling[25]. The same should be expected in the fly, and the
share of a target’s input that a pathway supplies indicates where anatomy is likely to be enough.
The 2% boundary is an empirical result of this model, obtained with the synaptic weight and
threshold of Shiu et al., not a value taken from the literature.
Bates et al. describe the architecture of this connectome from a linear measure of influence:
local loops from sensory to effector neurons, linked by ascending and descending neurons, with
the regions for learning and navigation supervising them[12]. The spiking map of Fig. 1f is the
dynamical counterpart of that analysis. It is sparser than the anatomy, 139 responding region
pairs against 358 connected ones, because a spiking network passes on only the input that reaches


                                                 9
threshold. The embodied results agree with the supervisory reading. Removing the mushroom
body removes approach and leaves walking and wall avoidance intact. A random lesion of the
same size leaves approach mostly intact (2.55 of 3) at a cost to walking. Removing neurons in
order of their synapse count stops the legs before any set of neurons specific to navigation can be
identified.
The body is coupled to the network by two hand-written rules with fixed constants, and they set
the scope of the embodied claims. The turn toward or away from an odour is computed from the
odour’s left-right difference and scaled by the valence, the balance between the MBONs that
drive approach and those that drive avoidance. The battery, the lesion, the rewiring and the
rescue test what the connectome supplies to that rule: the valence. That quantity reaches the
MBONs through four layers of measured connectivity, and it falls to less than a tenth of its
value when that connectivity is shuffled (0.11 against 1.41). Which MBONs the valence reads is
part of the rule. A sum over every MBON gives the same lesion, rescue and replay results and a
lower score for the rewired fly (3.5 of 32). The learning has no effect on the behaviour under
that readout (Supplementary Discussion; Supplementary Table 6). The rescue locates the deficit
of both manipulations at the mushroom-body output. It does not show that the connectome
computes the turn. The receptor drive has a fixed total rate and only its left-right difference
follows the plume, so the network is not asked to encode concentration. The gait is the body
model’s, with amplitudes set by the leg motor pools. Driving a grooming neuron therefore speeds
the fly up, and bilateral DNa02 drive, which lowers the leg drive in the wiring and slows the
animal, does not slow the body.
Three results are independent of the odour rule: the pathway tests outside the body, the
descending-neuron drives in the body, for which no steering rule exists, and the replay experiment.
Replay holds the motor commands fixed and removes only the feedback. Commands that reach
94% of the sources in closed loop reach 2% when replayed from a start rotated by 30◦ , and the
rotation alone does not lower the closed loop’s score. Navigation in this body depends on the
sensorimotor loop, whatever the origin of the commands. Locomotor control in animals likewise
combines feedforward commands with sensory feedback[73].
The antennal lobe is where reading the connectome at face value departs furthest from the
animal. The cholinergic local neurons exist in the fly[60]. Their excitation of projection neurons
there passes through electrical synapses and survives a block of chemical transmission[74],
although paired recordings also find a cholinergic component[61], and between glomeruli inhibition
dominates over excitation[75]. The model has no electrical synapses and reads each chemical
synapse of these cells as full-strength excitation. This closes a loop between projection and local
neurons that sustains itself once started, and two thirds of the Kenyon cells then fire to every
odour. Scaling that one class of synapse to 0.15 of its face value brings the Kenyon-cell code into
the fly’s range. The APL neuron, whose feedback keeps the code sparse in the animal[62], is in
the model with the wrong sign. The transmitter prediction makes both APL cells cholinergic, so
they excite the Kenyon cells. In the animal the APL is GABAergic[63], and the connectome’s
own annotation lists GABA as verified for it. We kept the prediction for every neuron rather than
correct cells one at a time, because the model’s claim is about the connectome as read; the APL
is the one cell whose predicted sign we know is wrong and tested. The verified column disagrees
with the prediction’s sign for 2,732 of the 60,448 neurons whose entry names one transmitter,
1.5% of all synapses, 82% of them in the optic lobe. The two cells, signed GABAergic in an
exploratory run (Methods), lower the share of Kenyon cells active, as read, from 68% to 42%,
short of the fly’s range, and applying every verified sign in the table gives the same 42%. What
the matched variant corrects is therefore the antennal lobe’s excitatory loop rather than the
APL’s sign.
The model’s prediction for learning is that depression of the Kenyon-cell output synapses is
associative when Kenyon-cell activity depends on the odour. A network whose Kenyon cells fire


                                                10
without the odour is devalued by reinforcement alone, however dense its code is while the odour
is present. Where the code does depend on the odour, generalisation between odours follows the
overlap of their codes, as it does in the fly[66, 34]. In the loop the depression of the input to
approach-driving MBONs tips the balance of MBON output toward avoidance, and the trained
fly turns away from the odour, as aversively trained flies do[41, 43]. That holds when the valence
is read as this balance. The same depression, read as a sum over every MBON, stops at a floor
set by the MBONs the Kenyon cells do not drive, a fifth of the naive value, and the behaviour
does not change (Supplementary Discussion).
The model makes as few assumptions as a spiking network can: point neurons with one sign
each, chemical synapses only, no neuromodulation, no spontaneous activity, one animal. Each
omission is known to matter. Neuromodulators reconfigure what a fixed wiring diagram does[16],
and the strength of a connection depends on the density and the location of its synapses as well
as on their number[76]. The battery and the pathway tests are fixed, so each omission can be
tested as one addition scored against the same measurements. Candidates are a background
matched to the activity that walking evokes across the fly’s brain[77], electrical synapses as they
are mapped, and synaptic weights that differ by transmitter. Another is a reinforcement signal
produced by the network’s own dopaminergic neurons, whose inputs include feedback from the
MBONs[78], in place of an imposed one. A causal model of the fly brain has been proposed that
uses the connectome as a prior for perturbation data[79]; the input-share rule and the functional
map indicate where that prior is already informative.
Three predictions follow for experiments in the animal. A pathway should drive its target when
it supplies a few per cent of the target’s input synapses and fail at a hundredth of that. Steering
by DNa01 and DNa02 should depend on ongoing activity in the leg motor network, because it
acts by lowering that activity on one side. A fly whose Kenyon cells are active without an odour
should be devalued by reinforcement that is not paired with the odour.


Methods

The connectome and the network model

We used the BANC connectome of the adult female Drosophila brain and ventral nerve cord[12]:
its neuron table, its connection list (neuron pairs with their synapse counts) and its per-neuron
transmitter prediction. Every neuron that appears in the connection list is simulated: 172,433
neurons joined by 11,752,828 connections that carry 35,733,096 synapses. Of these, 6,404 carry a
non-neuronal label in the table (6,281 glia, 83 trachea, 40 marked as not a neuron); they were left
in as units like the rest, hold 0.07% of the output synapses, and fire 0.003% of the spikes of an
embodied run. The connection list names each neuron by its identifier in the release it was built
from. The neuron table carries that identifier beside a newer one from the latest release, which
differs for 12,806 neurons whose segments changed between releases; the table is joined to the
list on the build identifier. No neuron, connection or synapse was added, removed or reweighted
except where a manipulation below says so. The list includes 156,311 connections from a neuron
onto itself (1,422,866 synapses, 4.0% of all), which were left in; they do not act on a neuron that
has a refractory period, because their input arrives 1.8 ms after the neuron’s own spike, inside
the 2.2 ms period, when synaptic input is discarded.
Each neuron is a leaky integrate-and-fire unit with the equations and parameters of the brain
model of Shiu et al.[22], who took them from earlier fly models and recordings except the synaptic
weight, their one free parameter, which they set so that sugar input drives the proboscis motor
neuron and showed robust to a 30% change; we retuned none of them. The membrane potential




                                                11
v and the synaptic variable g of each neuron obey
                                dv                      dg
                              τm    = v0 − v + g,    τs    = −g,                          (1)
                                 dt                     dt
with v0 = −52 mV, τm = 20 ms and τs = 5 ms. A spike is emitted when v exceeds −45 mV; v is
then reset to −52 mV, g to zero, and the neuron is refractory for 2.2 ms. A presynaptic spike
adds to the postsynaptic g, 1.8 ms later,
                                      ∆g = s n × 0.275 mV,                                      (2)
where n is the number of synapses of the connection (synapse counts approximate synaptic
contact area[80]) and s is the sign of the presynaptic neuron: +1 for acetylcholine, dopamine,
serotonin, octopamine and tyramine, −1 for GABA, glutamate and histamine. Glutamate is
signed as inhibitory, as in the brain model[22] and as measured in the fly’s antennal lobe[59].
The prediction is the one distributed with the connectome; predictions of this kind come from a
classifier trained on electron-microscopy images of synapses[14]. The 7,213 simulated neurons
without a prediction have their 10,000 connections (29,865 synapses) signed +1. The network
was simulated in Brian 2 (version 2.7.1)[81] at its default time step of 0.1 ms.
Input is delivered as independent Poisson spike trains, one per driven neuron. Each event
depolarises its target by 68.75 mV (250 × 0.275 mV), so the neuron fires at every event that
does not fall in its refractory period. The olfactory receptor neurons and the LC and LPLC
neurons, through which the body’s senses enter, have no refractory period and follow their drive
spike for spike, as do the other sensory receptor neurons in the runs of Fig. 1 f and Fig. 2; their
self-connections (66 synapses at most) stay below the roughly 160 synapses that fire a neuron
on their own. Every other driven neuron keeps its refractory period, so a drive of rate r gives
r/(1 + 0.0022 r) spikes per second: 259 at 600 Hz.
Populations are selected by the connectome’s labels. Of the 2,978 olfactory receptor neurons,
the 2,968 with a labelled nerve are driven, split by the side of the nerve they enter; the 4,192
visual projection neurons of the LC and LPLC types are split by the position of their root point
relative to the midline; the 104 mushroom-body output neurons (MBONs), the DNa02 pair and
the descending neurons are selected by cell type; the 391 leg motor neurons form six pools by leg
and side (63 to 70 neurons each). The mushroom-body set is the 4,504 Kenyon cells and the 104
MBONs. A lesion sets to zero every synapse onto or from the set. The rewired null permutes
the postsynaptic ends of all connections with a seeded random generator, so every neuron keeps
its in-degree, its out-degree and the sign and synapse count of each of its connections while
the identity of every partner is randomised; each trial seed gives a different permutation. A
pathway-scoped shuffle (Fig. 2 g) permutes the postsynaptic ends of one population’s outgoing
connections among themselves, leaving every other connection intact.
The 24 regions of Fig. 1 come from 13 groups defined by the connectome’s super-class, cell-class
and region labels (Supplementary Table 8 gives each group’s defining labels and neuron count;
the antennal-lobe projection and local neurons and the 8,409 neurons matching no rule fall in the
other central-brain group), then split the central complex by cell-type prefix into its neuropils,
the sensory group by cell class and the leg motor neurons by segment.

s with no other input, and spikes were counted in every neuron in 20 ms steps. The response
of a region is its mean rate per neuron over the episode; each of the 24 regions was driven in
turn, three seeds each. For display, each row was transformed as log(1 + rate) and divided
by its maximum. Rows and columns of Fig. 1 e and f follow average-linkage clustering of the
symmetrised log synapse counts.

Validation against pathways of known behavioural role

Each test of Fig. 2 drives a population with a known behavioural role and reads an identified
target neuron or motor pool; the rows are those of Fig. 2 a, b and h, cited in the Results. LC
and LPLC populations were driven at 40 Hz per neuron (LC4 also at 80, 150 and 250 Hz; LC16,
LC10a and LC10d also at 600 and 1,500 Hz); descending neurons, singly or as a type, and the
PFL3 and PFL1 populations at 600 Hz, which their refractory period turns into 259 spikes per
second; and the taste receptor neurons over 10 to 200 Hz, with the two-tastant grids scored at
200 Hz sugar. Each run lasted 3 s with spikes counted in 20 ms steps; the readout is the target’s
mean rate over the run, and for the descending rows the rate of each leg-motor pool by side.
Every driven condition was run with an undriven control at the same settings and with a twin
on the rewired network whose permutation seed equals the run seed; each had nine seeds. The
background ladder (Fig. 2 i) adds Poisson input to every neuron at 0, 2, 5, 10 or 20 Hz, and
effects are measured against the control at the same background.
A pathway conducts when the driven target’s rate exceeds its control’s by at least 0.10 Hz
and by the row’s guard: half the control’s own rate, or three seed-to-seed standard deviations
of the control for the sided rows. It is wiring-specific when the twin’s effect is under half the
intact effect. A sided row must also carry the sign measured in the animal, and a side is not
read when its total leg-motor drive is under 0.10 Hz. The outcome in Fig. 2 h is conducts,
wiring-specific and sign together. The gap-junction row adds one connection from the giant fibre
onto the tergotrochanteral motor neuron by hand, at 13 (the chemical count), 52, 104, 208 and
416 synapse equivalents, the only hand-placed connection in the paper. A pathway’s share of its
target’s input is its synapses onto the target over the target’s total input synapses.


The odour probe

The odour code (Fig. 3) was measured outside the body on the untrained network. The antennal
receptor neurons were driven at 80 Hz for 0.5 s, then silenced. Before each presentation the
network rested in silence for 1 s; an odour was then presented for 1 s by driving the receptor
neurons of its five glomeruli at 150 Hz, and every neuron’s spikes were counted. The main pair is
A = D, DA1, DA2, DA3 and DA4l against B = DA4m, DC1, DC2, DC3 and DC4; three further
pairs continue through the alphabetical list of glomeruli, from DL1 to VL1. For generalisation

                                                 13
(Fig. 3 h), A = DL5 and DM1 to DM4, and B shares k of them, its other glomeruli drawn from
DM5, DM6, DP1l, DP1m and V. A layer’s active fraction, the quantity on the figure axes, is the
fraction of its neurons with at least one spike during the presentation, and the text gives it as
a percentage, the share of the layer’s neurons active; the overlap of two odours is the fraction
shared between the 5% most active Kenyon cells of each; the MBON output is the total of
MBON spikes in the presentation, and an output under 5 spikes before training is treated as
unmeasurable.
The matched variant scales the output synapses of the 105 antennal-lobe local neurons predicted
cholinergic by a gain g. The sweep used g = 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.15, 0.1, 0.075, 0.05 and 0; the variant
used through the paper takes the largest gain at which the share of Kenyon cells active lies in 3
to 15% with an overlap of at most 0.5, a rule fixed before the sweep ran, which gives g = 0.15.
Training on odour A depresses each Kenyon-cell-to-MBON synapse by a fraction 0.5 (or 1.0)
of its Kenyon cell’s response to A normalised to the largest response, after which both odours
are presented again; the depression of an odour is 1 minus the ratio of its MBON output after
training to before. Silencing a type sets its output synapses to zero. The contingency test drives
every receptor neuron at 0, 5, 40 or 120 Hz with no odour, after the warm-up or from silence;
the breadth test builds odours of 5, 10 or 20 glomeruli; the drive test presents the odours at 0.5
and 0.25 of the 150 Hz rate. Six seeds per condition went to the gain sweep and the contingency
test, whose results set the matched gain and the bistability, and three to the other tests; the
seed sets only the Poisson input, and the seed-to-seed standard deviation of the share of Kenyon
cells active is 0.03 percentage points as read and 0.17 at the matched gain.
One run tested the sign of the APL and was not pre-registered. The two APL cells, the typed
right cell and the unlabelled left one, were signed inhibitory, as the table’s verified column
has them, and the odour code was measured as above, with and without training on odour
A, at gains of 1, 0.5, 0.25, 0.15 and 0.05 on the cholinergic local neurons’ output, three seeds
each. Two further variants silenced the two cells, and gave every neuron with a single verified
transmitter the sign of that transmitter, the unlabelled APL cell taking its twin’s. The run is in
the reproducibility bundle beside the registered sweeps, marked as exploratory.


The body and its coupling to the network

The body is NeuroMechFly[36, 38], a MuJoCo model of the fly, stepped at 0.1 ms with leg
adhesion on and contact sensors on the tibia and the five tarsal segments of every leg. Walking
uses flygym’s hybrid controller (a central-pattern-generator network with its retraction and
stumbling reflexes), changed only to take six amplitudes, one per leg.
Every 50 ms the loop runs once. The arena’s odour intensity at the two sensors on each side
of the head, antenna and maxillary palp, is combined as a weighted mean (weights 9 and 1);
the asymmetry a = (L − R)/mean, clipped to ±1, sets the receptor drive of the left and right
antennae to 120+80a and 120−80a Hz. From each eye’s ommatidia, 1 minus the mean brightness
over the upper 45% of rows is the looming signal; each side’s LC and LPLC neurons receive 250
times this value, capped at 150 Hz. The network then runs for 50 ms, and the spike counts of
each leg-motor pool, of the MBONs and of the left and right DNa02 are read.
The pool counts are smoothed by an exponential moving average with weight 0.15, and each
leg’s amplitude is 1 + 0.8 (c − b)/s, where c is the smoothed count, b the pool’s baseline and s
the mean baseline over pools, both measured over the last 17 of 25 calibration steps at 120 Hz
receptor drive before the episode and floored at 1; amplitudes are clipped to between 0.3 and
1.45. Steering scales the three legs of one side. The odour term is e = tanh(β 2 ) sign(β) with
β = −500 (L − R)/mean. The valence v is the spike count of the approach-driving MBONs minus
that of the avoidance-driving MBONs, divided by a fixed norm and clipped to between −1.5
and 1.5. When v is positive the legs on the side of the stronger odour are scaled by 1 − 0.8 |e| v;


                                                14
when it is negative the legs on the other side are scaled by 1 − 0.8 |e| |v|, and the fly turns away.
Each MBON type drives approach or avoidance according to its transmitter, after the finding
that activating a glutamatergic MBON repels flies and activating a GABAergic or cholinergic
one attracts them[41]: the transmitter is the table’s verified entry where the type has one, else
the published one[78] (MBON26, 27, 30 and 32), else the majority of the per-neuron predictions.
Of the 104 MBONs read, 75 drive approach, 25 avoidance and 4 are unsigned (MBON24 and
MBON29; Supplementary Table 5). The norm, 37.35 as read and 25.22 for the matched variant,
is the intact network’s mean balance over the calibration steps (53.78 and 36.32 spikes per step)
divided by 1.44, the intact fly’s untrained valence under the summed readout, so that the turn
rule has the same scale under both readouts. The summed readout reads v as the summed MBON
count divided by 107, clipped to between 0 and 1.5; its results are reported in the Supplementary
Discussion and Supplementary Tables 6 and 7. When the larger looming signal exceeds 0.05, a
ramp r rises linearly to 1 at 0.28: the odour term is multiplied by 1 − r, and the legs opposite
the stronger looming signal (or, when the two DNa02 counts differ, opposite the more active
DNa02) are scaled by 1 − 2.2 r. Side scalings are clipped to between 0.3 and 1.
Arenas are built on flygym’s odour arena[38], with one odour whose intensity falls with the
inverse square of the distance from each source; a source is consumed and switched off when the
fly’s centre comes within 2.5 mm of it (4 mm in the learning arena). Walls are boxes 1.2 mm
thick and 3 mm high. The fly starts at the origin facing +x. The layouts are ours. The nine
arenas are an open field, a single detour, two-, three- and four-wall slaloms, a wide gate, two
one-sided walls, a long S-curve and a dense slalom, with 32 sources in all and a 20 s cap. The
forage arena of Fig. 5 has three sources at (26, −6), (28, −9) and (24, −5) mm behind a wall at
x = 15 mm spanning y = −4 to 6 mm, and a 16 s cap. The learning arena has one source, at
(22, 11) mm for the model as read, and 6 s episodes. A seed sets the random generators of the
Poisson input and of the physics reset and, in rewired runs, the permutation.


The nine-arena battery

Each of 20 seeds ran every arena under each of three wirings, intact, rewired and mushroom-body-
lesioned: 180 runs per condition. The measures per run are the sources reached, the straightness
(net displacement over path length), the median speed over 50 ms steps and the fraction of steps
with speed above 1 mm/s. The chance level is a correlated random walk through the same nine
geometries: a heading perturbed every step, the intact fly’s own speed (the mean over its runs of
the median speed), reflection at walls, the 2.5 mm capture radius, the 20 s cap and 20 seeds. Two
reference controllers with the same speed, capture radius, cap and seeds are given the position of
every source and step straight toward the nearest one that remains; when a wall blocks a step,
one stays where it is and the other steps along the wall (Supplementary Table 1). The recordings
of Fig. 4 e, l and m are every neuron’s spike count in every 50 ms step of one run per condition
(arena i, seed 0).


Perturbations in the body

The forage-arena conditions (Fig. 5 b to d) are intact; the mushroom body lesioned; rewired;
the lesioned and the rewired fly with the valence pinned at 1.44, the intact fly’s mean under the
summed readout (1.41 with the balance); the lesioned fly pinned at 0 and at 0.7 and the intact
fly pinned at 0; a random lesion of 4,608 neurons, the size of the mushroom-body set; and the
intact fly with the looming signal set to zero. Each has 20 completed runs, taken in seed order;
runs that ended in a physics fault are excluded and counted, and extra seeds were run to fill the
20, under a rule written before they ran. The six measures are the sources reached, the mean
over sources of the closest approach, the mean DNa02 count per step, the number of steps within
0.5 mm of the wall, the path length and the mean leg-motor-pool rate.

Motor replay (Fig. 5 e, g) ran each arena and seed three times. A closed-loop run from the
standard start recorded its six amplitudes at every step. A second closed-loop run started with
the heading rotated by 30◦ . An open-loop run from the same rotated start fed the recorded
amplitudes to the body step by step while the network’s output was ignored.
Descending-neuron drive (Fig. 5 f, j) took place in an open arena with the receptor neurons at
120 Hz on both sides (also 40 and 0 Hz for j): after 1 s the target received 600 Hz for 2 s, then
1 s more without it. The targets were DNa01 and DNa02 on each side, DNa02 on both sides,
DNp09 on the left, the connectome’s threat-response cluster on both sides, DNg12 and DNp01
on the left as non-locomotor controls, and no target as the baseline; 20 seeds each intact, and
for the rewired rows three permutations with seven seeds each. Speed is path length over time,
in the drive window against the settling window (0.2 to 1 s), and the net turn is the change in
heading over the drive window.
Graded removal (Fig. 5 h, i) zeroes every synapse of a fraction of the candidate neurons: all
simulated neurons except the receptor, LC and LPLC, leg-motor, MBON and DNa02 sets that
the loop drives or reads. Random removal draws the set with the seed, at 5 to 90%; targeted
removal takes the neurons with the most synapses at 1 to 20%, over all candidates or over the
central-brain intrinsic neurons only; 20 seeds each.


Learning in the loop

Of the 15,523 connections from Kenyon cells onto MBONs, the plastic ones are the 8,377 onto
the MBONs of the compartments that the PPL1 dopamine neurons innervate, the compartments
of aversive learning (types MBON11 to 14, 16 to 20, 23, 25, 28, 30 and 32 to 35, 50 cells, after
the hemibrain compartment map[78]; mean weight 0.513 mV). The other 7,146 keep their weight.
At every 50 ms step each plastic connection’s eligibility trace decays with a 200 ms time constant
and gains its Kenyon cell’s spikes in the step. While the reinforcement signal is on, the connection
accumulates a depression of η times its eligibility times its MBON’s spikes in the step; at the
end of the episode the accumulated depression is subtracted from the weight, which is clipped to
between 0 and its initial value. Under the summed readout with all 15,523 connections plastic
(Supplementary Discussion) the learning lowers the valence in both conditions, but not below
a floor set by MBONs the Kenyon cells do not drive, and 19 of 20 trained flies still reach the
source. Two further arms change one of the two at a time (Supplementary Fig. 4; Supplementary
Table 7): the balance readout with all 15,523 connections plastic, and the summed count (divided
by 107 as read and by 81 in the matched variant, as in the Supplementary Discussion) with the
8,377. Each ran the three conditions below with 20 seeds in both models, and its predictions
were written down before it ran.
A run is a naive test episode, six training episodes and a post-test episode, each 6 s from the
same start, with the source switched on at the start and off at contact. In the paired condition
the reinforcement is on throughout each training episode while the fly forages in the odour.
In the unpaired condition the reinforcement is on but the odour is withheld, both antennae
receiving 5 Hz, so the Kenyon cells are silent. In the no-plasticity condition η = 0. The main
arm used η = 0.02 and 20 seeds per condition; the learning-rate ladder ran the paired condition
at eleven values of η from 10−5 to 0.08, 20 seeds each. The measures are the mean weight of the
plastic connections (0.513 to 0.138 mV after pairing as read; a mean over all 15,523 connections,
which includes those that cannot change, reads 0.500 to 0.298 mV), the valence averaged over an
episode, the closest approach to the source in the post test, and the devaluation, the fall from the
naive to the post test in the fraction of the start distance closed. The matched variant (Fig. 6
h, i) is the g = 0.15 network of Fig. 3. Its balance norm, 25.22, was set as above from its own
intact runs, and its source was placed at (23, 8) mm by a rule fixed before the runs: the as-read
source’s distance from the start, side and margin, transferred to the variant’s own path with the


                                                16
valence forced to zero. The registered tests of the variant are on the behaviour: the depression is
associative when the paired and unpaired flies’ devaluation differ (P < 0.01, Cliff’s δ ≥ 0.5) with
the unpaired valence at or above 0.7, and expressed when more than half of the trained flies miss
the source by more than 4 mm while the unpaired and unplastic flies reach it.


Statistics and reproducibility

The unit of replication is the seed, one simulated fly with its own input and initial conditions;
arenas are repeated measures on a fly. All tests are two-sided. The replay result (Fig. 5 g) is a
paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test on each fly’s mean fraction of sources reached over the nine
arenas, computed exactly (SciPy 1.17.1, exact method): W = 0, P = 1.9 × 10−6 for 20 flies.
Group comparisons (Fig. 6 d, e, h, Supplementary Table 7, and the rewired against the lesioned
fly in Fig. 4 f) use the Mann–Whitney U test with Cliff’s δ (the probability that a value from
one group exceeds one from the other, minus the reverse) as the effect size, and 95% intervals
from 5,000 percentile-bootstrap resamples, reported in the Results where the two groups overlap
(at δ = ±1 every resample gives the same value). The power of a 20-versus-20 Mann–Whitney
test at α = 0.05 against a normal shift whose δ is 0.2, 0.35, 0.5 or 0.65 is 0.19, 0.49, 0.83 and 0.97
(2,000 simulations each). The correlation of Fig. 3 i is Spearman’s ρ over 11 types (ρ = 0.78,
P = 0.0045); without the largest type it is 0.71 (P = 0.022), and leaving out any one type gives
0.71 to 0.88. No correction for multiple comparisons was applied; a Bonferroni correction across
the six learning tests changes no conclusion.
Every sweep’s cells, metrics, thresholds and predictions were written down before it was launched,
except the exploratory APL run above, which is marked as such where it is cited. Simulations
ran in one container (Ubuntu 22.04, Python 3.10.12, Brian 2 2.7.1 with Cython 0.29.37, flygym
1.2.1, MuJoCo 3.2.7[83], NumPy 2.0.2[84], SciPy 1.15.3[85]), one run per 2-core, 12 GiB pod
on the National Research Platform’s Nautilus cluster; figures and statistics were built locally
(Python 3.11, SciPy 1.17.1).

Supplementary Discussion. The learning experiment with the valence read as the
summed MBON count

The summed readout takes the valence as the spike count of all 104 MBONs in a 50 ms step divided
by 107, clipped to between 0 and 1.5. The runs reported here let the reinforcement reach all 15,523
Kenyon-cell-to-MBON connections. Six training episodes with the odour paired with the reinforcement
took the mean weight from 0.500 to 0.128 mV and the valence from 1.44 to 0.24. The behaviour did not
change. The trained fly’s closest approach to the source in the post test was a median 0.23 mm, against
0.20 mm before training. The trained flies still reached the source, 19 of 20, as did 20 of 20 without
plasticity (P = 0.23 and 0.47 against no plasticity on closest approach and devaluation, two-sided
Mann–Whitney U tests). The valence after training fell to between 0.24 and 0.25 at learning rates
from η = 3 × 10−4 upward and no further, and 18 to 20 of 20 flies reached the source at every rate.
The floor was MBON output that the plasticity cannot lower. The MBON spikes per step fell from 155
to 26 with training, and ten MBONs carried 87% of the 26. Six of the ten take at most a quarter of
their input from Kenyon cells (MBON19, two MBON26 cells, MBON04 and two MBON31 cells). The
two MBON05 cells take a third and a half and are driven besides by the APL and the glutamatergic
MBON01. The two that are mostly driven by Kenyon cells (MBON11 and MBON06) are the two that
fell most. A valence pinned at 0 abolishes approach in the forage arena and one pinned at 0.7 keeps it
(Fig. 5). The switch of the learning arena lay below 0.24: 18 to 20 of 20 flies with a valence between
0.24 and 0.28 reached the source. In the matched variant the depression was associative at the synapse:
pairing took the weight to 0.182 mV and the valence to 0.25, while reinforcement alone left the weight
at 0.495 mV and the valence at 1.42. Its trained fly still reached the source (19 of 20), because its
valence fell to the same floor.
A sum over every MBON cannot fall below the output of the MBONs that learning does not touch, and
it cannot change sign. MBON types in the fly differ in the behaviour they drive, and learning shifts
the balance between those that drive approach and those that drive avoidance (Methods). The main
text therefore reads the valence as that balance and confines the reinforcement to the compartments of
aversive learning (Methods; Supplementary Table 5). The balance readout, its predictions and a pilot
of 150 runs were written down before the in-body runs of Figs 4 to 6 were made. Supplementary Table
6 sets the two readouts side by side. The lesion, the rescues, the pinned conditions and the replay give
the same results under both. Two other results differ between the two readouts. The intact fly stops
less often at walls under the balance readout (30.25 against 29.20 of 32 sources in the nine arenas, and

                                                  32
all three sources of the forage arena in 20 against 16 of 20 seeds). The rewired fly reaches more sources
under the balance readout (5.80 against 3.50 of 32). Its MBONs fire as much under both readouts,
about 7.5 spikes per step, and the balance readout reads that as a valence of 0.11 where the summed
readout reads 0.07, because 75 of the 104 MBONs drive approach.
The analysis of the main text differs from the summed readout’s in two things: the readout, and which
connections the reinforcement reaches. Two further arms change one of them at a time, each with the
three conditions, 20 flies and both models of the learning experiment, and with predictions written
down before the runs (Supplementary Fig. 2; Supplementary Table 7). With the summed readout and
the reinforcement confined to the aversive compartments, the valence after pairing is 0.83 (0.84 in the
matched variant). It stays above the floor of 0.24 because the MBONs of the other compartments
keep their output, and all 20 flies reach the source in every condition. With the balance readout
and the reinforcement in every compartment, the depression lowers the approach-driving and the
avoidance-driving MBONs alike, to 13.7 and 12.5 spikes per step as read (10.0 and 10.5 in the matched
variant). The valence after pairing is 0.03 (−0.02), and the trained fly then neither approaches nor
avoids. It passes the source at a median 11.2 mm (10.2 mm), farther than every fly without plasticity
and nearer than every trained fly of the main text’s analysis (P = 6.8 × 10−8 for each comparison in
each model). The source is still reached on the way by 2 of 20 flies (1 of 20). One registered prediction
missed by one fly: three earlier runs with the valence held at zero had put that count between 2 and
12, and the matched variant’s 1 of 20 falls below it. Reinforcement alone does what pairing does in the
network as read and changes nothing in the matched variant, in both arms as in the main text. The
learned avoidance needs both changes. The trained fly turns away only when the readout is signed and
the reinforcement is confined to the aversive compartments, where 41 of the 50 MBONs drive approach.
