SUMMARY

How do neural circuits coordinate multiple behavioral responses to a single sensory cue? Here, we investi-
gate how sweet taste drives appetitive behaviors in Drosophila, including feeding, locomotor suppression,
spatial preference, and associative learning. We find that neural circuits mediating different innate responses
to sugar are partially overlapping and diverge at the second and third layers. Connectomic analyses reveal
distinct subcircuits that mediate different behaviors. Connectome-based simulations of neuronal activity
predict that second-order sugar neurons act synergistically to promote downstream activity and that bitter
input overrides the sugar circuit through multiple pathways acting at third- and fourth-order neurons.
Consistent with the latter prediction, optogenetic experiments suggest that bitter input inhibits third- and
fourth-order sugar neurons to override the sugar pathway, whereas hunger and diet act earlier in the circuit
to modulate behavior. Together, these studies provide insight into how circuits are organized to drive diverse
behavioral responses to a single stimulus.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND SUBJECT DETAILS

Fly strains and maintenance
Flies were reared at 25 C on standard cornmeal or cornmeal/molasses food and maintained in constant darkness to avoid activation
of Chrimson. All experiments used mated females, which were collected within a few days after eclosion. Flies were placed on food
containing 1 mM all trans-retinal for 2–4 days prior to testing. During starvation, flies were housed in vials with 1 mM all trans-retinal
solution on a piece of Kimwipe. The duration of starvation was 1 day except for experiments with bitter GRN activation in Figures S1H
and S1I, which also tested 2-day starvation. The high-sugar diet consisted of standard fly food with 30% sucrose (w/v) added, and
flies were fed on a high-sugar diet or control diet (standard fly food) for 6–7 days. Our standard fly food uses the Janelia recipe (0.7%
agar, 8.0% cornmeal, 2.0% inactivated yeast, 5.0% molasses, 0.50% propionic acid, 0.20% Tegosept).
   Genotypes used for each experiment are specified in the figures. Detailed information regarding fly strains is provided in the key
resources table, including full genotypes for split-Gal4 lines if this information was available. For interneuron activation, multiple split-
Gal4 lines were available for some neurons. We selected initial lines for testing based on the strength of their proboscis extension
phenotype in Shiu et al.8 and the specificity of their expression pattern, based on images in the Janelia FlyLight Split-Gal4 database
(https://splitgal4.janelia.org/) acquired by Sterne et al.31 We initially tested one split-Gal4 line per neuronal type; if phenotypes were
borderline in any behavioral assay we then tested a second line labeling the same neuron (if available). Each neuronal ‘‘type’’ com-
prises a single neuron per hemisphere with the exception of Bract, which consists of 2 cells, but only one cell appears to be labeled in
the split-Gal4 lines.

METHOD DETAILS

Behavioral assays
Assays for locomotion, innate preference, and associative learning were conducted using previously described methods.30 The
behavioral arena contains a circular chamber with a glass cover, and flies are filmed from above using a USB camera. The infrared
light for illumination and the red (627 nm) LED array for optogenetic stimulation are located just beneath the chamber. For locomotor
and preference assays, low, medium, and high intensity corresponded to 4, 20, and 35 mW/mm2. 20–25 flies were tested per trial.
Flies were loaded into the chamber using mouth aspiration and given 3 min to habituate before the experiment started. Flies
were filmed at 30 frames/s.
   Locomotion and innate preference were assayed sequentially in the same flies. To quantify locomotor effects, light stimulation was
presented for 5 s. Each of the three light intensities was delivered to the same flies in increasing order of intensity, with at least 2 min
between stimulations to ensure that the behavior had recovered. After an additional rest period, the same flies were then tested for
innate preference by delivering low intensity light stimulation to two opposing quadrants for 30 s. The flies then had a 30 s rest period
without light, followed by low intensity light stimulation in the other two quadrants for 30 s. Switching the light quadrants ensured that
we could assess light preference independently of any spatial bias. This protocol was then repeated sequentially with medium and
high intensity light.
   For associative learning, the odors used were 3-octanol (1:1000) and 4-methylcyclohexanol (1:750). The flow rate was 100 mL/min.
Which odor was used as the CS+ (paired with light) or CS- (not paired with light) was counterbalanced across trials. 4 mW/mm2 was
initially used for learning experiments with Gr64f-Gal4, as this was the intensity that elicited preference in the innate choice assay, but
we also tested 30 mW/mm2 in Figure S1B. 30 mW/mm2 was used to activate Gr5a-expressing neurons (Figure S1G) or downstream
sugar circuit interneurons (Figure S4C).
   All learning assays used 1 min odor-light pairing, following the protocol used in previous learning studies,55 other than the exper-
iments shown in Figure S1B which used 3 3 10 s pairings. For 1 min pairing, the CS + odor was presented in all quadrants for 1 min

along with light stimulation (thirty 1 s pulses) that started 5 s after odor valve opening. After a 1 min rest, the CS- odor was presented
alone for 1 min. Following another 1 min rest, the CS+ and CS- odors were simultaneously delivered to different sets of opposing
quadrants for 1 min, allowing the flies to choose between the odors. After another 1 min rest, the two odors were presented again
for 1 min but the odor quadrants were switched to control for any spatial bias.
   For 10 s odor-light pairing, the CS+ odor was presented for 10 s with light stimulation (three 1 s pulses) that started 10 s after odor
valve opening, following the protocol used in a recent study.41 The CS- odor was presented for 10 s without light. The CS+ and CS-
presentations were repeated three times (with CS+ and CS- interspersed) with 2 min between each odor onset. Odor preference tests
were performed in the same way as for the 1 min pairing protocol, with flies given 1 min to choose between odors over two separate
tests.
   Fly videos were analyzed using FlyTracker,56 and FlyTracker output was analyzed in MATLAB to quantify locomotion or preference.
Preference index (PI) values were quantified in 1 s bins. For innate preference, PI was calculated as (# flies in light quadrants – # flies in
non-light quadrants)/total # flies. For learning, PI for the conditioned odor was calculated as (# flies in CS+ quadrants – # flies in CS-
quadrants)/total # flies. The final PI shown in the figures represents the average PI over the last 5 s of light or odor presentation. Data
from the first and second tests were combined, so two trials were obtained for each set of flies.
   To analyze locomotion, forward and angular velocities were averaged over 0.33 s bins (10 frames) except for the zoomed-in plots
shown in Figures 2A and 2B. (The zoomed-in traces may show higher peak values than the non-zoomed plots for this reason.) To
quantify locomotor changes at light onset or throughout the light period, we averaged forward or angular velocity over the first 1 s
or entire 5 s of light presentation, respectively, and subtracted these values from the baseline values averaged over the 4 s preceding
light onset. Quantifying the light-evoked change in locomotion was more meaningful than quantifying absolute velocity during light
because different groups may show different baseline velocities, which could be misinterpreted as an optogenetic effect if the change
from baseline is not considered. Differences in baseline velocity complicated the interpretation of some results (Figures 5B and 5C),
which is mentioned in the figure legends. For a small number of experiments in which some trials had a very low baseline that made
locomotor suppression difficult to assess, we excluded trials with baseline velocity less than 2 mm/s.
   For testing co-activation of bitter GRNs with Foxglove and Bluebell in Figure 5C, we noticed that baseline velocity of the co-acti-
vation genotype decreased during the course of the experiment, especially if the flies were previously stimulated with light. We there-
fore we repeated the locomotor assay while only testing flies with medium intensity light (i.e., excluding the low intensity trial that
normally comes first), and we additionally excluded trials of any genotype with baseline velocity less than 2 mm/s. This resulted in
a dataset where co-activation flies did not show impaired baseline velocity, enabling us to confirm that co-activation causes loco-
motor suppression.
   Although we initially tested the effect of activating each sugar interneuron on locomotion, preference, and learning, for follow-up
experiments testing neuronal silencing (Figure S4E), modulation by hunger state (Figure 3E), or modulation by diet (Figure 3F), we
focused on analyzing locomotor suppression driven by Rattle, FMIn, or Fdg because these phenotypes represented the most robust
behaviors elicited by sugar interneurons.
   Proboscis extension assays were conducted in 1-day starved flies as previously described.33,57 Flies were immobilized on their
backs with myristic acid wax, and the two anterior pairs of legs were glued down so that the proboscis was accessible. Flies recov-
ered from gluing for 30–60 min in a humidified chamber and were water-satiated before testing. To activate neurons with Chrimson, a
red LED (617 nm) was manually turned on for 1 s. Each fly was given two chances to respond to the light stimulus. A similar protocol
was used to test for light suppression of sugar-evoked proboscis extension except that sucrose and light were delivered simulta-
neously. At the end of each experiment, flies that did not respond during the experiment were tested with a positive control
(500 mM sucrose) and were excluded from analysis if they did not respond.

Immunostaining
Immunostaining experiments were performed as previously described.30,39 Briefly, brains were dissected in phosphate buffered sa-
line (PBS), fixed for 15–20 min in 4% paraformaldehyde, washed multiple times with PBS containing 0.3% Triton X-100 (PBST),
blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 1 h, incubated with primary antibody at 4 C for 2–4 days, washed in PBST, incubated
with secondary antibody at 4 C for 1–2 days, washed in PBST and PBS, and mounted in Vectashield. We used rabbit anti-DsRed
(1:500) as the primary antibody and Alexa Fluor goat anti-rabbit (1:500) as the secondary antibody. Images were acquired on an
Olympus spinning-disk confocal microscope (Clavicle, Rattle, G2N-1, Foxglove+Bluebell, Mime, and Marge) or a Keyence BZ-
X810 microscope (all other neurons). Images were processed using FIJI. We did not stain peripheral tissues or the ventral nerve
cord, but nerve cord staining for most of the split-Gal4 lines can be viewed at the FlyLight split-Gal4 expression database
(splitgal4.janelia.org).

Calcium imaging
Calcium imaging methods were similar to those previously described.33,39 Briefly, flies were immobilized on clear tape in an imaging
chamber and an imaging window was cut on the anterior surface of the head, with the antennae removed. The brain was immersed in
modified artificial hemolymph in which 15 mM ribose is substituted for sucrose and trehalose.13 Images were acquired at 10 Hz
using a two-photon laser scanning microscope (Bruker) with a Ti:S laser (Coherent) with an excitation wavelength of 925 nm and
a 20x water-immersion objective. Tastants were delivered to the proboscis using a custom-built solenoid pinch valve system

controlled by MATLAB software, and tastants were removed by a vacuum line controlled by a separate pinch valve. Proper taste
delivery was monitored using a side-mounted camera. At least 1 min rest was given between trials to avoid habituation. Calcium im-
aging data were analyzed using MATLAB code from previous studies.33,39 The average pixel intensity within a region of interest was
calculated for each frame, the average fluorescence over 2 s prior to taste delivery was used as the baseline signal, and the DF/F
(change in fluorescence divided by baseline) for each frame was then calculated.

Connectome analysis
Annotated neuronal connectivity from a whole-brain dataset34,35,58 was downloaded from FlyWire using version 630. Each of the
sugar circuit interneurons has been previously identified and labeled in this connectome.8 To examine the targets and output simi-
larity between second-order neurons, and to make the diagram in Figure 3H, we focused on neurons in a single hemisphere (left). To
determine which second-order neurons provide input to Fdg, Bract, Foxglove, or Bluebell, we analyzed inputs to cells in both hemi-
spheres. Cell IDs are: Zorro (720575940629888530), Rattle (720575940638103349), FMIn (720575940614763666), G2N-1
(720575940620874757), Clavicle (720575940655014049), Phantom (720575940616103218), Usnea (720575940632648612), Fdg
(720575940631997032, 720575940647030324), Bract (720575940645045527, 720575940610001220, 720575940626557442,
720575940627285267), Foxglove (720575940611160930, 720575940628755815) and Bluebell (720575940631168057, 720575
940635166571). To quantify the output similarity between two neurons we used cosine similarity, a metric that has been previously
used in connectomic analyses.59 We calculated the cosine of the angle between the vectors containing the number of synapses onto
each output neuron. These vectors are n-dimensional, where n is the total number of output neurons for the two input neurons.
   For the diagram in Figure 3H, arrow thickness represents connection strength: thin = 5-9 synapses; medium = 10–19 synapses;
thick = 20+ synapses. For simplicity, the diagram omits predicted inhibitory connections (from Phantom and Usnea), feedback con-
nections from 3rd to 2nd order neurons, and weak connections from FMIn to Foxglove and Rattle to Bluebell that are only observed in
one hemisphere.

Whole-brain simulations
Brain simulations were conducted in Python based on code provided publicly by Philip Shiu (https://github.com/philshiu/Drosophila_
brain_model), and details about the model are described in Shiu et al. (2023).37 Code for all simulations in this paper can be found on
GitHub (https://github.com/avdevineni/sugar-circuit). Simulations were based on connectivity data in version 630 of the Flywire con-
nectome. We used the default parameters for the model, which were chosen by Shiu et al.37 based on published experimental data
and include: 52 mV resting potential, 52 mV reset potential after a spike, 45 mV spiking threshold, 2.2 ms refractory period, 5 ms
tau for synapse decay, 1.8 ms time delay from spike to change in membrane potential, and 0.275 mV synaptic weight. In all simu-
lations, neurons were stimulated for 1 s and activity of neurons in the left hemisphere was quantified. Neurons were activated or
silenced bilaterally with the exception of GRNs, because only sugar and bitter GRNs on the left side have been thoroughly annotated
and we did not want to risk activating incorrect neurons. All simulations were repeated over 30 trials, and mean values were quan-
tified. Simulations were performed with multiple stimulation intensities (typically 50, 100, and 200 Hz), corresponding to a similar
range as tested in Shiu et al.37 Results from the most informative intensity are shown in Figure 4, and additional results are shown
in Figure S5 or reported in Table S1. In Figures 4A and 4B, we tested activation or silencing of every individual second-order neuron
and every pair of neurons; for combinations of 3 or more neurons, we focused on only testing the excitatory neurons.

Figure S4, related to Figure 3. Additional characterization of behavioral roles of sugar interneurons
(A) Change in forward speed at light onset elicited by activation of each sugar interneuron at each light intensity (n = 7-12 trials).
Both Zorro lines caused very brief locomotor suppression (see Figure 3B) at medium and high intensity that was not always
significant when quantifying velocity over the first 1 sec, but was highly significant when quantifying velocity over the first 0.33 sec
(p<0.001 for both lines at medium and high intensities). Locomotor suppression elicited by Zorro was also stronger than it appears
in Figure 3B because the traces show binned velocity over 0.33 sec bins.
(B) Innate positional preference elicited by activation of each sugar interneuron at each light intensity (n = 14-24 trials, 7-12 sets of
flies). Bar graphs show final PI. Some lines whose final PI was not significant caused statistically significant preference at earlier
time points, including Fdg (1) (significant preference with high intensity light at ~13-17 sec after light onset), Fdg (2) (most time
points from 11-24 sec after light onset; see Figure 3C), and Bract (1) (all time points from 9-15 sec after light onset; see Figure
3C), as quantified by two-way ANOVA followed by Dunnett’s post-tests.
(C) Learned odor preference elicited by activation of each type of sugar circuit interneuron (n = 16-24 trials, 8-12 sets of flies).
(D) Given the overlap between neurons that affected locomotion and innate preference, we asked whether the effects of
interneuron activation on different behaviors were correlated. The heatmap shows correlation values (Pearson’s r; asterisks
denote significance) between behavioral effects elicited by interneuron activation, including average data from all experimental
genotypes tested in Figure S4A-C (n = 10 for correlations with learning and n = 15 for all other correlations). L, M, or H denote
low, medium, or high intensity light, respectively. Values for “locomotion” represent the change in forward speed at light onset, so
negative values represent locomotor suppression. Locomotor suppression and positional preference were modestly correlated at
some light intensities, suggesting that these behaviors are mediated by overlapping circuits (and may also be intrinsically related,
even though they are dissociable), whereas no other pair of behaviors showed a significant correlation.
(E) Change in forward speed at light onset elicited by activation of sugar GRNs (Gr5a-lexA driving Aop-Chrim) while silencing
downstream sugar interneurons using Kir2.1 (n = 8-12 trials). For all three interneurons, experimental flies with both GRN
activation and downstream silencing (red bars) did not significantly differ from both of the controls with GRN activation only (blue
bars), indicating that neuronal silencing did not impair locomotor suppression.
(F) Analyzing second- to third-order connectivity using output similarity, which takes connection strength into account, revealed
the same two subcircuits as analyzing the number of shared targets in Figure 3G. The heatmap shows cosine similarity of outputs
for each pair of second-order neurons. Cosine similarity ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values representing more similar outputs.
Neurons are colored orange or red if they affected a single behavior or multiple behaviors, respectively.
Flies were one-day starved for all behavioral experiments. In panels A-C, black and grey bars represent the Gal4/+ and UAS/+
controls, respectively, and colored bars represent experimental flies carrying split-Gal4 and UAS-Chrim; colors represent which
layer of the circuit the interneuron resides in (see Figure 3H). Second-order neurons are listed in descending order of the number
of synapses they receive from sugar GRNs (as reported in Shiu et al., 2022 [S4]). Numbers in parentheses after the neuron name
denote which split-Gal4 line was used.

      Figure S5, related to Figure 4. Additional results from computational simulations of sugar circuit activity
      (A) We simulated the activation of sugar interneurons along with P9, a neuron that promotes walking, and quantified activity in
      downstream targets of P9 identified in Sapkal et al. [S5]. Neurons were activated at 200 Hz. Of six known P9 targets, only oDN1
      was strongly suppressed by activation of sugar GRNs; activity of the other neurons is not shown here. oDN1 activity was strongly
      suppressed by activating Foxglove, Bluebell, or Fdg, and was moderately suppressed by activating Rattle or FMIn.
      (B) Sugar and bitter GRNs were co-activated at 100 Hz (left) or 200 Hz (right). Histograms show the effect of bitter GRN activation
      on the activity of sugar-responsive neurons. Activation at 50 Hz is shown in Figure 4C.
      (C) Sugar GRNs were co-activated with putative downstream bitter neurons Scapula, Mime, or Marge at 50 Hz (left) or 100 Hz
      (right). Activation of Scapula, Mime, or Marge suppressed the sugar-evoked activation of specific third-, fourth-, or fifth-order sugar

Figure S6, related to Figure 5. Further characterization of phenotypes with activation of neurons in the sugar and bitter
pathways
(A) Co-activating bitter GRNs with second- or third-order sugar neurons caused either strong aversion or intermediate effects on
positional preference, depending on the neuron and stimulation intensity (n = 19-24 trials, 10-12 sets of flies). Traces show
preference with medium intensity stimulation. However, reduced aversion in the co-activation genotype should be interpreted
cautiously because these flies showed very low baseline velocity (see Figure 5B), which may prevent the flies from readily moving
between the light and non-light quadrants.
(B) Marge did not show any clear GCaMP responses to bitter taste (10 mM denatonium) delivered to the proboscis. Trace shows
the average response for one example fly (n = 6 trials) out of 7 flies imaged.
(C) Activation of Mime did not elicit positional preference or aversion (n = 12-16 trials, 6-8 sets of flies).
(D) Activation of Mime did not cause proboscis extension and did not significantly suppress proboscis extension evoked by sugar
GRN activation (left; n = 26-34 flies) or natural sugar (right; n = 34 flies). We anecdotally observed that PER evoked by natural
sugar may be less vigorous with light stimulation, but this was not quantified.
(E) Activation of Marge did not significantly affect locomotion (blue) or impair the ability of sugar GRN activation to cause
locomotor suppression; in fact, locomotor suppression seemed to last longer with Marge co-activation (n = 8-10 trials). Velocity
traces for medium intensity stimulation are shown, and bar graph shows change in velocity at light onset.
(F) Activation of Marge did not elicit positional preference or aversion (n = 20 trials, 10 sets of flies).
(G) Activation of Marge caused moderate proboscis extension at medium and high intensities and did not suppress proboscis
extension evoked by sugar GRN activation (n = 26 flies).
Flies were one-day starved for all behavioral experiments. Data in panels A and E were analyzed by one-way ANOVA followed by
Bonferroni’s post-tests, and data in panels D and G were analyzed by Fisher’s exact test. Fdg line (2) was used for co-activation
experiments.
